Onboarding Relaunch: Q1 Plan

You picked the right problem and showed your math, which most plans don't. I wouldn't send it yet. You're asking leadership for a yes with no price on it, and your 40% target is higher than anything you've hit, including your best cohort ever.

Don't send it yet
What I took this to be for

You want Ledgerly's leadership team to approve the Q1 onboarding relaunch, a five-email sequence plus an in-app checklist, so Growth can start in January. The decision on the table is yes or no on that start. I went full depth because it's going to leadership and the ask rests on several numbers. If it's an early heads-up rather than a request for sign-off, the missing price stops being a deal-breaker and the verdict becomes “fix these first.” The other flags stand.

I assumed this.

Where I'd push back

Worst first.

This will kill it
01

It asks for a yes and never says what it costs

What caught my eye
Approve the relaunch so we can start in January. We'll share final costs once the design work is scoped.Ask
Why I don't buy it

This is a huge red flag. It'll stop this dead in its tracks if you send it out.

  • You're asking leadership to approve spend they can't see. Design, the email build and engineering time on the checklist all cost something.
  • The only owner named is “Growth.” Nobody's name is on the checklist, and it needs engineering.
  • The upside is priced to the dollar ($365K). The cost side is blank, so nobody can weigh one against the other.

The first question in the room will be “what does it cost?” The page answers “later,” and that usually gets you “come back when you know.”

What I'd do

Put a number and a name on it before it goes. If design isn't scoped yet, give a range, or change the ask to approving the scoping work.

Ask →

Approve the relaunch so we can start in January. It will cost [$ range] for design and the email build, plus [engineering weeks] of engineering time, owned by [owner]. We'll confirm the final number by [date].

This will cost you credibility
02

Your 40% target beats the best cohort you've ever had

What caught my eye
The new sequence will lift first-week activation from 22% to 40% by the end of Q2.Summary, para 2our highest-activating cohort ever (the October 2025 webinar cohort) reached 35% activation.What we know
Why I don't buy it

I get where you're going, but you're reaching too far from where you are, and it isn't grounded in anything you can hit yet.

  • Your best cohort ever reached 35%, and those were webinar attendees who chose to show up. You're promising 40% across every new signup.
  • Only 31% open the first email, about 2,790 people a month. If the emails carry the lift, 1,620 more activations means 58% of those openers newly connecting a bank.
  • You wrote “will,” but this is a bet. Nothing in the plan says why this sequence beats what you've done before.

Someone in the room will call you on this. Once they do, they'll discount every other number on the page.

What I'd do

Keep the ambition, but call 40% a stretch goal, say what it rests on, and give the number you'd call a win.

Summary, para 2 →

We're aiming to lift first-week activation from [baseline]% to 40% by the end of Q2. Our best cohort so far reached 35%, so 40% is a stretch goal. We'd count [X]% as a clear win.

This will cost you credibility
03

The $365K counts every new activation as a customer paying for a full year

What caught my eye
At our average of $19 per month, that's about $365K in new annual recurring revenue (ARR) per monthly cohort.Impact
Why I don't buy it

I don't buy the $365K, and here's why.

  • 1,600 × $19 × 12 is $364,800, so the sum is right. It assumes all 1,600 pay $19 a month and stay the whole year.
  • Your summary says activated customers are “3x more likely to still be paying after six months.” So some of them stop paying, and a full year for all 1,600 is too generous.
  • The plan never says what share of new signups pay at all. If some are on a trial or a free plan, the number drops again.

Anyone who checks revenue for a living will spot this on the first read and cut your number for you.

What I'd do

Run the revenue on the share of activated customers who actually pay and stay, using your own retention data.

Impact →

If [X]% of the extra activated customers pay $19 a month and [Y]% are still paying after 12 months, that's about [$ amount] in new ARR per monthly cohort.

This will cost you credibility
04

You're treating numbers that move together as proof, and the rollout can't test it

What caught my eye
Connecting a bank account is what keeps customers.Summary, para 1NPS rose from 38 to 47 after we previewed the checklist in the September newsletter.What we knowWeek 7: launch to 100% of new signups.The plan, step 3
Why I don't buy it

I don't buy that connecting a bank account is what keeps people. The 3x could just mean your most committed customers are the ones who connect.

  • If serious customers are the ones connecting, pushing everyone else to connect won't buy you the same retention.
  • NPS asks how likely someone is to recommend the product or company. A rise after a newsletter preview lines up in time. It doesn't show anyone loves the checklist.
  • Launching to 100% leaves you nothing to compare against. Activation already moved from 22% to 24% without a relaunch, so you won't know what the relaunch did.

Someone will ask how you'll know it worked, and right now the page has no answer.

What I'd do

Say it as a belief you'll test, and hold back a slice of new signups so the test means something. Cut “Customers love the checklist idea” unless you have feedback on the checklist itself.

Summary, para 1 →

Customers who connect a bank account in week one are 3x more likely to still be paying after six months. We think getting more people to connect will improve retention, and the relaunch will test that against a [share]% holdout group.

What caught my eye
only 22% of new signups connect a bank account in their first weekSummary, para 1Activation today: 22% (see summary). Activation for the most recent full month was 24%.What we know

  • The 22% line cites the summary as its source, so it's pointing at itself.

This will cost you credibility
06

Half the plan waits on a billing migration with no date

What caught my eye
Weeks 2–6: build the in-app checklist. Engineering will pick this up once the billing migration ships.The plan, step 2
Why I don't buy it

This is the question that sinks the timeline. When does the billing migration ship?

  • The checklist build starts in week 2, so the plan assumes the migration is done by then. Nothing on the page says it will be.
  • If it slips, the checklist slips, and the week 7 launch either slips too or goes out with emails only.

Leadership probably knows the migration's real status better than this page does. If it's running late, they'll read the whole timeline as wishful.

What I'd do

Give the migration's ship date and say what you'll do if it slips.

The plan, step 2 →

Weeks 2–6: build the in-app checklist. This needs the billing migration, due [date]. If it slips, we'll [fallback].

This will cost you credibility
07

You open by claiming you're the only app for freelancers, and you're not

What caught my eye
Ledgerly is the only bookkeeping app built for freelancers.Summary, para 1
Why I don't buy it

This is flat wrong, and it's the first line leadership reads.

  • Intuit says QuickBooks Solopreneur is “designed for self-employed individuals running a one-person business.”
  • Xero's page is titled “Self-Employed Accounting Software for Freelancers.”
  • Zapier's roundup of self-employed accounting software lists eight options.

Nothing in the ask depends on this line, but anyone can check it in a minute. Once they do, they'll wonder what else you rounded up.

What I'd do

Cut the superlative and say what actually sets you apart.

Summary, para 1 →

Ledgerly is a bookkeeping app built for freelancers, and [what sets it apart].

A few more things

  • “Biggest problem” needs a comparison. Nothing on the page ranks activation against anything else, like churn or the cost of getting signups. Show the ranking or say “a big problem.”
  • Say which part does the lifting. The summary credits “the new sequence” for the 40%, but the plan has two parts. Say what you expect from the emails and what from the checklist, so you know which one to keep.

What's working

  • The decision is right at the top. “Decision needed: Approve the Q1 onboarding relaunch” leaves no doubt what you want.
  • You show your math. 9,000 × 18% is 1,620, and the ARR sum checks out from your own figures.
  • One metric with a date on it, first-week bank connection by the end of Q2. Keep this. It's good.
  • Putting your best cohort ever (35%) on the page is honest. Use it to set the target instead of leaving it there to argue against you.
